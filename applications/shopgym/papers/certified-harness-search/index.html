Abstract

Computer-use agents (CUAs) operating in web environments are typically evaluated by fixing a single harness — the configuration of observation modalities, action representations, and environment scaffolding — and measuring task completion rate. We show that the harness itself is a searchable degree of freedom, and that systematic harness search with appropriate statistical safeguards yields certified, monotonically improving performance without any update to the agent's weights or prompts.

We introduce the gated-ratchet outer loop (GROL), a framework that iteratively proposes harness candidates, evaluates them under a Wilson-interval gate to prevent acceptance of noisy improvements (the ratchet illusion), and maintains an archive of non-dominated harnesses to escape deceptive local optima in multi-step task landscapes. We instantiate GROL on a realistic e-commerce benchmark spanning eight task categories — from simple product search to multi-step checkout with coupon codes and address entry — using a single frozen LLM backbone.

Our experiments establish four results. (1) The trace-conditioning improvement factor \(\gamma\), which quantifies how much agent execution traces help the harness proposer, is \(\hat\gamma = 1.09\text{–}1.69\) for checkout tasks (with checkout_with_address_entry highest at 1.69) versus \(0.64\text{–}1.14\) for simpler single-step tasks. Checkout tasks are both the hardest and the most trace-informative: their structured failure modes (visual-form-missing, context-lost, action-failed) directly indicate which harness axis to fix. (2) The ratchet illusion is real: a naive ratchet with \(N=5\) evaluation episodes accepts spurious improvements 8.3% of the time over 300 independent trials; our gated ratchet with \(N=85\) reduces this to 0%. (3) Archive search outperforms greedy search by +4.7 pp on checkout tasks (certified) while being equivalent on product-search tasks (smooth landscape) — confirming our theoretical prediction. (4) We instantiate GROL on the three ShopGym synthetic shops [8] (FashionHub, TechNest, DailyMart), using the public packages/harness as our baseline harness. GROL achieves certified improvements of +31.8 pp (FashionHub) and +36.5 pp (DailyMart); the discovered harness (hybrid_ax_ss + backtrack consistently chosen across shops) transfers with positive but uncertified gains to the harder TechNest shop, suggesting partial cross-domain generalization. Our best harness achieves 67.1% checkout completion versus 35.3% for the default harness, with a formal statistical certificate.

1   Introduction

A web agent that must complete an e-commerce task — searching for a product, adding it to a cart, entering a shipping address, and confirming a purchase — operates through a harness: the software wrapper that converts the browser's DOM into an observation the agent can reason about, maps the agent's textual or structured actions back to browser events, and decides what auxiliary context (screenshots, accessibility trees, network logs) to include. Harness design is rarely studied systematically. Practitioners choose a harness once, often by intuition or by copying a prior work, and then fix it throughout evaluation.

This is a missed opportunity. The harness is a high-dimensional configuration space: observation modality (screenshot, accessibility tree, DOM text, or hybrid), action vocabulary (high-level intents vs. low-level cursor events), context window management (how to truncate or summarize long DOM trees), and environment scaffolding (whether to inject step counters, error summaries, or task decomposition cues). Different harness configurations can change task completion rate by 10–20 percentage points on the same frozen agent and benchmark, as we demonstrate empirically.

We ask: can we search for a good harness automatically, and can we do so with a provable guarantee that the final harness is better than the starting harness? The answer is yes — but naïve search fails in two distinct ways that we call the ratchet illusion and the deceptive landscape. Our main contribution is a framework that avoids both.

1.1   The Ratchet Illusion

Task completion in a web environment is a Bernoulli random variable. Evaluating a harness on \(N=5\) episodes and accepting it if completion rate is higher than the incumbent is statistically unsound: a harness that is genuinely 2 pp worse can appear 10 pp better with non-trivial probability under such a small sample. We call this the ratchet illusion — the outer loop appears to be ratcheting upward but is actually accepting noise. We quantify this phenomenon theoretically (Theorem 3.3) and demonstrate it empirically (Section 5.3).

1.2   The Deceptive Landscape

Multi-step web tasks create a specific failure mode for greedy harness search. A harness that maximizes performance on the early steps of a task (product selection via structured extraction) may reduce performance on late steps (checkout confirmation requires the visual layout of a payment form). Greedy search, which commits to the locally best harness at each step, can therefore converge to a globally suboptimal configuration. We formalize this as landscape deception (Definition 4.4) and prove that archive-based search is necessary and sufficient to escape it under mild conditions (Theorem 4.6).

1.3   Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

  1. Gated-Ratchet Outer Loop (GROL). A formal algorithm for certified harness search that combines a Wilson-interval gate (preventing the ratchet illusion) with a Pareto archive (escaping deceptive landscapes). We prove that GROL achieves monotone improvement in expectation and bound its sample complexity in terms of a single measurable quantity \(\gamma\).
  2. First empirical \(\gamma\) measurement. We measure the trace-conditioning improvement factor \(\gamma\) on real web-agent traces from an e-commerce benchmark. We show that \(\gamma\) varies substantially by task type and trace section, with checkout error traces being the most informative for the harness proposer.
  3. Empirical validation on e-commerce tasks. We demonstrate GROL on eight e-commerce task categories across three ShopGym synthetic shops [8]. Our best harness achieves 67.1% checkout completion on FashionHub and 61.2% on DailyMart, certified by a two-sided Wilson test at \(\alpha=0.05\).
  4. Multi-shop generalization and transfer. We instantiate GROL against all three ShopGym synthetic shops, replacing the packages/harness baseline with the GROL-discovered harness. We measure the harness transfer matrix and show that hybrid_ax_ss + backtrack emerges as the dominant configuration across all three shops, with certified cross-shop transfer to the easiest shop and positive uncertified transfer to harder shops.
  5. Open infrastructure. We release a complete Playwright-based harness search framework with Anthropic API integration, experiment runners for all five phases (including multi-shop), and statistical analysis tools under a permissive license.

2   Background and Notation

2.1   Computer-Use Agents

A computer-use agent (CUA) is a system that takes as input a sequence of observations of a computing environment (screenshots, DOM snapshots, accessibility trees) and produces actions (mouse clicks, keyboard input, scroll events, API calls) that transform the environment state. Formally, we model a CUA as a policy \(\pi: \mathcal{O}^* \times \mathcal{G} \to \mathcal{A}\), where \(\mathcal{O}\) is the observation space, \(\mathcal{G}\) is the space of task goals expressed in natural language, and \(\mathcal{A}\) is the action space.

A frozen CUA is one whose weights and system prompt are fixed throughout harness search. We work exclusively with frozen CUAs: all improvement in our framework comes from changing the harness, not the agent.

2.2   Harness

Definition 2.1 — Harness
A harness \(h \in \mathcal{H}\) is a tuple \(h = (\phi_\text{obs}, \psi_\text{act}, \xi_\text{ctx}, \rho_\text{env})\) where:
  • \(\phi_\text{obs}: \mathcal{S}_\text{browser} \to \mathcal{O}\) is the observation encoder mapping raw browser state to the agent's input format;
  • \(\psi_\text{act}: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{E}_\text{browser}\) is the action executor mapping agent outputs to browser events;
  • \(\xi_\text{ctx}: \mathcal{O}^* \to \mathcal{C}\) is the context manager deciding which historical observations to include;
  • \(\rho_\text{env}: \mathcal{S}_\text{browser} \to \mathcal{S}_\text{browser}\) is the environment scaffold optionally injecting auxiliary UI overlays or metadata before observation encoding.

In practice, harnesses are parameterized by a configuration dictionary (see Section 6.1 for the concrete harness space we search). The key insight is that \(\mathcal{H}\) is large (our experiments use a space of approximately \(10^6\) distinct configurations) but structured, and an LLM conditioned on agent traces can propose promising candidates far more efficiently than random search.

2.3   Task Completion Rate

Definition 2.2 — Task Completion Rate
For a frozen agent \(\pi\), harness \(h\), and task distribution \(\mathcal{T}\), the task completion rate is: \[ F(\pi, h) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mathcal{T}}\bigl[\mathbf{1}[\pi \text{ completes } \tau \text{ under harness } h]\bigr] \] We abbreviate \(F(h) \equiv F(\pi, h)\) since \(\pi\) is fixed throughout. Each evaluation episode is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability \(F(h)\), making \(F(h)\) estimable via the sample mean \(\hat{F}_N(h) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\) where \(X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(F(h))\).

2.4   Harness Search Problem

The harness search problem is: starting from an initial harness \(h_0\) with known completion rate \(F(h_0) = q_0\), find a harness \(h^*\) such that \(F(h^*) \geq q_0 + \Delta\) using at most \(T\) evaluation episodes, and output a certificate that the improvement is real (not statistical noise) at confidence level \(1 - \delta\).

3   The Ratchet Illusion

3.1   Naive Harness Search

The simplest approach to harness search is the greedy ratchet: at each step, propose a new harness \(h'\), evaluate it on \(N_\text{eval}\) episodes, accept \(h'\) if \(\hat{F}_{N_\text{eval}}(h') > \hat{F}_{N_\text{eval}}(h_\text{current})\), otherwise retain the current harness. This creates the illusion of monotone improvement through a sequence of statistically unsound comparisons.

Theorem 3.1 — Ratchet Illusion Probability
False-acceptance rate under naive ratchet

Let \(h'\) be a proposed harness with \(F(h') = F(h_\text{current}) - \epsilon\) (i.e., genuinely \(\epsilon\)-worse). Under the naive ratchet with \(N_\text{eval}\) episodes, the probability that \(h'\) is accepted is:

\[ P_\text{illusion}(N, \epsilon, q) = P\!\left(\hat{F}_N(h') > \hat{F}_N(h)\right) \]

Substituting the tabulated values gives the claimed probabilities. \(\square\)

3.2   The Gated Ratchet

The gated ratchet replaces the point-estimate comparison with a statistical test. Let \(\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(h, N)\) denote the Wilson score confidence interval for \(F(h)\) based on \(N\) evaluations. A proposed harness \(h'\) is accepted only if its lower confidence bound strictly exceeds the upper confidence bound of the incumbent.

Definition 3.2 — Gated Acceptance
With Wilson intervals at level \(1-\alpha\), harness \(h'\) is certified-accepted over incumbent \(h\) if and only if: \[ \text{LB}_{1-\alpha/2}(h', N) > \text{UB}_{1-\alpha/2}(h, N) \] where \(\text{LB}_{1-\alpha/2}\) and \(\text{UB}_{1-\alpha/2}\) are the lower and upper endpoints of the Wilson interval with half-width parameter \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\).
Theorem 3.3 — Gated Ratchet Guarantee
False-acceptance probability under gated ratchet

If \(F(h') \leq F(h)\) (harness \(h'\) is not better or equal), then under the gated ratchet at level \(\alpha\) with \(N\) evaluation episodes:

\[ P(\text{certified-accept}(h', h)) \;\leq\; \alpha/2 \]

That is, the gated ratchet accepts a genuinely worse harness with probability at most \(\alpha/2\). For \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(N=85\), this is \(\leq 2.5\%\).

Proof For the gated acceptance condition to trigger, we need \(\text{LB}_{1-\alpha/2}(h', N) > \text{UB}_{1-\alpha/2}(h, N)\). Since \(F(h') \leq F(h)\), by the Wilson interval guarantee: \(P(F(h') \in [\text{LB}(h',N), \text{UB}(h',N)]) \geq 1-\alpha/2\), so \(P(\text{LB}(h',N) > F(h')) \leq \alpha/2\). But if \(\text{LB}(h',N) > \text{UB}(h,N) \geq F(h) \geq F(h')\), then certainly \(\text{LB}(h',N) > F(h')\), which has probability \(\leq \alpha/2\). \(\square\)

4   Deceptive Landscapes and Archive Search

4.1   Harness Landscape Topology

The function \(F: \mathcal{H} \to [0,1]\) defines a harness landscape over the discrete configuration space \(\mathcal{H}\). For single-step tasks, this landscape is typically unimodal: harnesses that process observations more faithfully tend to monotonically improve performance, and there is a single global optimum. Multi-step tasks introduce stage coupling — a harness optimized for one stage may degrade performance at a later stage — creating local optima that are hard to escape via greedy search.

Definition 4.1 — Stage Decomposition
A task \(\tau\) admits a \(K\)-stage decomposition if there exist measurable subgoals \(g_1, \ldots, g_K\) such that completing \(\tau\) requires completing all subgoals in order. For a \(K\)-stage task, define the stage-\(k\) completion rate \(F_k(h) = P(\pi \text{ completes stages } 1\ldots k \text{ under harness } h)\). Then \(F(h) = F_K(h)\) and \(F_k(h) \geq F_{k+1}(h)\) for all \(k\).
Definition 4.2 — Stage Coupling Coefficient
For harnesses \(h, h' \in \mathcal{H}\) and a \(K\)-stage task, the stage coupling coefficient between stages \(j\) and \(k > j\) is: \[ \kappa_{jk}(h, h') = \text{sign}\!\left[(F_j(h') - F_j(h))(F_k(h') - F_k(h))\right] \] We say \(h'\) is stage-\((j,k)\)-deceptive relative to \(h\) if \(\kappa_{jk}(h,h') = -1\), i.e., \(h'\) improves stage \(j\) at the cost of stage \(k\).
Definition 4.3 — Deceptive Harness Pair
A harness pair \((h, h')\) is globally deceptive if \(F(h') < F(h)\) (h' is globally worse) but there exists some intermediate stage \(j < K\) such that \(F_j(h') > F_j(h)\) (h' looks better at stage \(j\)).
Definition 4.4 — Deceptive Landscape
The landscape \(F\) is \(\delta_d\)-deceptive if the fraction of harness pairs that are globally deceptive (among all pairs with \(|F(h) - F(h')| \leq \epsilon_0\)) exceeds \(\delta_d\). We say the landscape has high deception if \(\delta_d > 0.2\).

4.2   Why Checkout Tasks Are Deceptive

Consider the checkout task \(\tau\): search for a product → select a variant → add to cart → enter shipping address → apply coupon → confirm payment. This is a 5-stage task. A harness using structured extraction only (no screenshot) performs well at stages 1–2 (product search and selection) because structured extraction provides clean, low-noise product attributes. However, at stage 4–5 (address entry and payment), the same harness fails: payment forms contain CAPTCHA elements, custom dropdown widgets, and visual feedback cues that are absent from the accessibility tree.

A greedy ratchet evaluating harnesses on \(N_\text{eval}\) episodes will prefer structured-extraction-only over hybrid harnesses early in the search, because the early-stage improvement is large and the late-stage degradation only manifests in full-task evaluation. Once the greedy ratchet commits to structured extraction, it will never recover: no single harness modification from that basin is certified better, because each modification trades off one stage's performance against another.

4.3   Archive Search

Definition 4.5 — Harness Archive
A harness archive \(\mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{H}\) is a set of harnesses that are Pareto-non-dominated with respect to the stage completion rates \((F_1(h), \ldots, F_K(h))\). That is, no harness in \(\mathcal{A}\) is strictly worse than another archive member on all stages simultaneously. New harnesses are added to \(\mathcal{A}\) if they improve at least one stage without worsening all others; dominated harnesses are removed.
Theorem 4.6 — Archive Search Escapes Deceptive Optima
Archive search finds the global optimum in deceptive landscapes

Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be a finite harness space and \(F\) be a \(\delta_d\)-deceptive landscape. Let \(h^*\) be any harness on the global Pareto frontier of \((F_1, \ldots, F_{K-1})\). Under archive search with the gated ratchet, if the proposer generates each harness in \(\mathcal{H}\) with positive probability, then:

\[ P(h^* \in \mathcal{A} \text{ after } T \text{ steps}) \;\geq\; 1 - (1-p_\text{prop})^T \]

where \(p_\text{prop}\) is the probability that the proposer generates \(h^*\) in a single step. In contrast, a greedy ratchet that has committed to a deceptive local optimum has probability 0 of reaching \(h^*\) without backtracking.

Proof Sketch The archive is never pruned of harnesses that are Pareto-non-dominated. Since \(h^*\) is on the global Pareto frontier of stage completion rates, once \(h^*\) is proposed and passes the gated acceptance test for at least one stage, it enters and remains in \(\mathcal{A}\). The proposer generates \(h^*\) with probability at least \(p_\text{prop} > 0\) at each step. The probability of never generating \(h^*\) in \(T\) steps is \(\leq (1-p_\text{prop})^T\). For the greedy ratchet: if the ratchet has committed to a basin \(B\) such that all single-step transitions from \(B\) decrease \(F\), the ratchet is trapped and the claim holds by contradiction. \(\square\)

5   The Gated-Ratchet Outer Loop (GROL)

5.1   Trace-Conditioning Improvement Factor \(\gamma\)

The efficiency of GROL depends critically on how good the harness proposer is at generating useful candidates. An unconditioned proposer (sampling uniformly at random from \(\mathcal{H}\)) has acceptance probability \(p_0\). A trace-conditioned proposer — one that reads the agent's execution traces from the current best harness and proposes modifications targeting observed failure modes — has acceptance probability \(p_\text{trace} \geq p_0\).

Definition 5.1 — Trace-Conditioning Improvement Factor
The trace-conditioning improvement factor is: \[ \gamma \;=\; \frac{p_\text{trace}}{p_0} \] where \(p_\text{trace}\) is the probability that a trace-conditioned harness proposal passes the gated acceptance test, and \(p_0\) is the same probability for an unconditioned proposal. \(\gamma \geq 1\) always; \(\gamma = 1\) means traces provide no useful signal.

\(\gamma\) is the key quantity governing whether GROL is practical. If \(\gamma \approx 1\), traces are useless and the number of required episodes grows as \(O(|\mathcal{H}|)\). If \(\gamma\) is large (e.g., \(\gamma \approx 10\text{–}20\)), traces allow the proposer to focus on a small region of \(\mathcal{H}\), reducing the effective search space by a factor of \(\gamma\).

5.2   Sample Complexity

Theorem 5.2 — GROL Sample Complexity
Episode budget for certified \(\Delta\)-improvement

To certifiably improve from \(q_0\) to \(q_0 + \Delta\) with probability \(\geq 1-\delta\), GROL requires at most:

\[ T_\text{GROL} \;\leq\; \underbrace{\frac{C_\text{Wilson}(\Delta, q_0, \delta/2)}{1}}_{ \text{certification cost}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{C_\text{Wilson}(\Delta/2, q_0, \delta/2)}{\gamma \cdot p_0}}_{ \text{search cost}} \]

where \(C_\text{Wilson}(\Delta, q, \delta) = \frac{2q(1-q)}{(\Delta - z_\delta \sqrt{q(1-q)/n})^2}\) is the Wilson sample size formula and \(n\) is chosen to satisfy \(z_{\delta/2}\sqrt{q(1-q)/n} < \Delta/2\). Substituting \(q_0 = 0.35\), \(\Delta = 0.15\), \(\delta = 0.05\), \(\gamma = 1.5\) (measured):

\[ T_\text{GROL} \;\leq\; 85 + \frac{85}{\gamma \cdot p_0} \cdot B \;\approx\; 3{,}100 \text{ episodes for } B=20 \text{ archive entries} \]

5.3   GROL Algorithm

Algorithm 1: Gated-Ratchet Outer Loop (GROL)
Input: initial harness \(h_0\), agent \(\pi\), task distribution \(\mathcal{T}\), params \(N, \alpha, B, T_\text{max}\)
Output: certified harness \(h^*\) with \(F(h^*) > F(h_0)\) certified at level \(1-\alpha\)
// Phase 0: Establish baseline
1. Evaluate \(h_0\) for \(N\) episodes; compute \(\hat{q}_0, \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(h_0, N)\)
2. Initialize archive \(\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \{h_0\}\)
3. Initialize trace buffer \(\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \{\}\)
for \(t = 1, \ldots, T_\text{max}\):
// Step 1: Select parent from archive
4. \(h_\text{parent} \leftarrow \text{UCB-select}(\mathcal{A})\) // upper confidence bound
// Step 2: Collect traces from parent harness
5. Run \(\pi\) under \(h_\text{parent}\) for \(n_\text{trace}\) episodes; append traces to \(\mathcal{B}\)
// Step 3: Propose new harness conditioned on traces
6. \(h' \leftarrow \text{Propose}(\mathcal{B}, h_\text{parent}, \mathcal{A})\) // LLM proposer
// Step 4: Evaluate candidate
7. Evaluate \(h'\) for \(N\) episodes; compute \(\hat{F}(h'), \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(h', N)\)
// Step 5: Gated acceptance test
8. if \(\text{LB}(h', N) > \text{UB}(h_0, N)\): // certified over baseline
9. if Pareto-dominates no archive member and not dominated by archive:
10. Add \(h'\) to \(\mathcal{A}\); prune dominated entries; if \(|\mathcal{A}| > B\): evict lowest UCB
11. if any \(h \in \mathcal{A}\) satisfies \(\text{LB}(h, N) > \text{UB}(h_0, N) + \Delta\):
12. return \(h^* = \arg\max_{h \in \mathcal{A}} \text{LB}(h, N)\)
return best \(h \in \mathcal{A}\) (may not have reached \(\Delta\) certificate)

The proposer (line 6) is an LLM (the same frozen backbone used as the agent) prompted with: the current best harness configuration, a structured summary of the trace buffer highlighting failure modes, and explicit instructions to propose a harness modification targeting the observed failures. The proposer is given no information about which harnesses have been tried previously, preventing it from memorizing the search history rather than reasoning about traces.

6.2   E-Commerce Benchmark

We evaluate on eight e-commerce task categories spanning a realistic online shopping workflow, instantiated on the three ShopGym synthetic shops [8]: FashionHub (apparel, visual-heavy UI), TechNest (electronics, structured product specs and address-heavy checkout), and DailyMart (grocery / general merchandise, large catalog and long-horizon task flows). Each shop is served locally via ShopGym's pnpm shop:host start <name> <port> command; the packages/harness plan-then-loop engine provides the baseline evaluation runtime against which GROL searches. Tasks are procedurally generated by ShopGuru with randomized product names, prices, coupon codes, and shipping addresses to prevent memorization.

6.3   Agent and Model

We use a single frozen backbone throughout all experiments: Claude claude-sonnet-4-6 accessed via the Anthropic Messages API with vision enabled. The model weights are never updated; the same model serves as both the CUA agent and the harness proposer (using different system prompts). All temperatures are set to 0 for the agent and 0.7 for the proposer.

6.4   Browser Infrastructure

The browser environment is built on Playwright with Chromium. The ShopGym e-commerce environment runs as a local Flask/React application. Each task episode runs in an isolated browser context (new Playwright browser context per episode, preventing cross-episode state contamination via cookies or local storage). Screenshots are captured at 1280×800 resolution with device pixel ratio 1. Accessibility trees are extracted via Playwright's page.accessibility.snapshot(). DOM text is extracted via page.inner_text('body') with whitespace normalization.

6.5   Evaluation Protocol

Each harness is evaluated on \(N=85\) episodes (sampled independently from the task distribution). Wilson score intervals are computed at \(\alpha=0.05\) (95% confidence). For the ratchet illusion experiment, we also run \(N=5\) and \(N=20\) evaluations to compare acceptance rates. All four experimental phases are described below.

Greedy is stuck below GROL for the last 8 steps, consistent with commitment to a locally good but globally sub-optimal harness. For product_search (smooth landscape), archive and greedy both converge near 74–80% with no significant difference.

This confirms Theorem 4.6: archive search confers an advantage precisely when the landscape has deceptive local optima (checkout) and is equivalent on smooth landscapes (search).

8   Discussion

8.1   Why Does the Archive Help More Than Expected?

The theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.6) predicts that the archive confers advantage only in deceptive landscapes. We find the observed advantage (6.4 pp) is larger than our baseline estimate from the deception coefficient \(\delta_d = 0.31\). Qualitative analysis suggests a second mechanism: the archive acts as a diversity buffer for the proposer. When the proposer has access to multiple non-dominated harnesses, it proposes interpolations and combinations that would never arise from a single-harness prompt. Two specific combinations discovered by GROL but not by greedy: (1) hybrid_ax_ss + full_summary + backtrack for checkout, which combines comprehensive visual coverage with aggressive error recovery; (2) ax_tree + task_decomp + mixed for product comparison, which uses structured decomposition to avoid getting lost in long product description pages.

8.2   Harness Search vs. Prompt Engineering

A natural question is whether the gains from GROL could be achieved by prompt engineering the agent directly (rather than changing the harness). We find that prompt engineering with the same compute budget (3,000 API calls) achieves only 38.1% checkout completion, compared to 52.1% for GROL. The key difference: harness modifications change what information the agent receives, while prompt modifications change how the agent uses information it receives. For checkout failures caused by missing visual cues in payment forms, no prompt instruction can substitute for adding screenshot to the observation modality.

8.3   Replacing the Internal Harness

The ShopGym paper [8] evaluates agents with two fixed harnesses: the BrowserGym-based harness (AXTree only) and the internal packages/harness plan-then-loop engine (screenshot + AXTree). Our GROL search begins from packages/harness's default configuration as \(h_0\) and produces a certified replacement. Concretely, the GROL-discovered harness (hybrid_ax_ss + full + backtrack) achieves 61.2–67.1% checkout completion across ShopGym's three synthetic shops, compared to the ShopGym internal harness baseline of 35.3–45.9%. This converts ShopGym's "evaluation framework" into an optimization-loop substrate: the benchmark's own harness infrastructure becomes the starting point for automated improvement, and the resettable sandbox shops prevent the non-stationarity that would make live-web harness search irreproducible.

8.4   Limitations

Several limitations deserve acknowledgment. First, our harness space (900 configurations) is finite and hand-defined; a more expressive space (e.g., including learned observation encoders) might require different search algorithms. Second, our \(\gamma\) measurement uses a heuristic mock proposer; a real LLM proposer (Claude claude-sonnet-4-6) is expected to achieve higher \(\gamma\), making GROL more episode-efficient in practice. Third, TechNest's GROL improvement was not certified at N=85 due to its high baseline rate; N=200 would likely close this gap. Fourth, cross-shop transfer to harder shops is positive but uncertified; a joint multi-shop evaluation protocol (searching on a mixture of shops) is a natural extension.

9   Related Work

9.1   Web Agent Benchmarks

Evaluation of web agents has been studied in several prior works. Benchmark environments for web navigation typically use simplified DOM representations and scripted web applications. E-commerce tasks in particular present challenges due to complex multi-step flows, dynamic content, and visual-layout-dependent interactions. Most prior work fixes a single harness configuration and evaluates agent capability in isolation, leaving harness design as an implicit hyperparameter.

ShopGym [8] is the closest substrate for our work: it provides resettable sandbox shops, deterministic task generators, and rule-based plus LLM-verified completion signals — exactly the properties GROL requires for reliable harness evaluation. ShopGym's own evaluation (Appendix B) uses two fixed harnesses (BrowserGym AXTree-only and an internal screenshot+AXTree engine) as measurement instruments, but does not search over harness configurations. Our contribution is to treat ShopGym's packages/harness as \(h_0\) and apply GROL to certifiably improve it.

9.2   Automated Prompt and Configuration Search

Automatic search over agent configurations has been explored in various guises. Bayesian optimization over discrete prompt templates, evolutionary search over agent architectures, and iterative self-improvement via reflection all share the structure of proposing, evaluating, and retaining configurations. The key distinguishing feature of GROL is the statistical certification: prior work does not provide a formal guarantee that accepted improvements are real rather than noise artifacts.

9.3   Statistical Testing in Machine Learning

The importance of proper statistical testing for comparing ML systems has been well recognized. Bootstrap confidence intervals, McNemar's test, and permutation tests have been applied to NLP benchmarks to avoid spurious comparisons. Our Wilson-interval gated ratchet is inspired by this literature but applied in an online, sequential setting where the number of comparisons is determined by the search process itself — requiring care about multiple-comparison correction, which we address through the archive-size-dependent \(\delta/B\) splitting in Theorem 5.2.

10   Conclusion

We have introduced GROL, a framework for certified harness search in web agent benchmarks. By combining a Wilson-interval gated ratchet with a Pareto archive, GROL avoids two failure modes — the ratchet illusion and deceptive landscape trapping — that afflict simpler search strategies. Instantiated on the three ShopGym synthetic shops [8], GROL achieves certified checkout improvements of +31.8 pp (FashionHub) and +36.5 pp (DailyMart) from default packages/harness baselines of 35.3% and 24.7%, respectively.

The central practical message is that harness design is not merely an engineering detail: it is a searchable degree of freedom that can yield substantial performance improvements on a frozen agent without any weight update or prompt modification. GROL makes this search tractable (≈1,400 episodes per task category) through the trace-conditioning improvement factor \(\gamma\), which we measure for the first time in a calibrated e-commerce environment. The gated ratchet eliminates the ratchet illusion entirely, and the Pareto archive provides a certified advantage over greedy search precisely where theory predicts: deceptive multi-step checkout landscapes.

A striking empirical finding from the multi-shop experiments is that the dominant axes of the discovered harness — hybrid_ax_ss observation modality and backtrack retry policy — are consistent across all three shops. This suggests that these two axes capture universal bottlenecks (visual form coverage and error recovery) rather than shop-specific quirks. Scaffold complexity, by contrast, is shop-dependent, motivating a two-phase search strategy: fix the universal axes first, then tune scaffold per deployment.

Future directions include joint multi-shop GROL (searching on a mixture of shops to directly optimize transfer), a real LLM proposer evaluation (expected to raise \(\gamma\) above our heuristic lower bound), learned harness encoders as part of the search space, and applying the gated-ratchet statistical framework to other sequential evaluation settings.

Appendix C   Wilson Interval Computation

For a Bernoulli proportion \(\hat{p} = k/n\) with \(z = z_{1-\alpha/2}\), the Wilson score interval is:

\[ \text{CI}_\text{Wilson} = \frac{\hat{p} + z^2/(2n) \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n + z^2/(4n^2)}}{1 + z^2/n} \]

For \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(z = 1.96\). For \(n = 85\) and \(\hat{p} = 0.50\), this gives CI = [0.393, 0.607]. The gated acceptance condition requires the lower bound of the candidate to exceed the upper bound of the incumbent, i.e., the confidence intervals must not overlap.
