One Loop, Many Names

Heuristic Learning, GEPA, autoresearch, Meta-Harness, SkillOpt — five systems, five vocabularies, one mathematical object. The theory that explains them already exists; it's just scattered across five literatures that have never been introduced to each other.

There is a family of systems that improve without touching a single weight. A coding agent maintains a Python game policy into Deep-RL territory. A reflection loop evolves prompts that beat reinforcement learning with 35x fewer rollouts. An overnight ratchet edits a training script while its author sleeps. An outer-loop agent rewrites the scaffolding around a frozen model and tops a competitive leaderboard. An optimizer makes bounded edits to a skills file and gains twenty-four points on Codex.

Each arrived with its own vocabulary — heuristic systems, reflective evolution, research loops, harness optimization, skill training. Reading them side by side, the suspicion becomes hard to shake: this is one thing wearing five costumes. This essay makes that suspicion precise, and argues that the mathematics needed to understand it largely exists already — in search theory from the 1970s, in evolutionary computation, in bandits, in information theory, and in a handful of remarkable papers from the last eighteen months that nobody has yet assembled into a picture.

The object

Strip the branding and every one of these systems is the same tuple:

Formally: stochastic search over a discrete artifact space, where the proposal distribution is a frozen pretrained model conditioned on execution traces, under noisy black-box evaluation. Once you say it that way, five mature literatures raise their hands.

Lens one: this is universal search with a learned prior

In 1973, Leonid Levin proved something that should be on the first slide of every talk in this field. If solutions are efficiently verifiable, there is a search procedure — enumerate programs in order of prior probability, budgeted by runtime — whose total cost is, up to a constant, optimal:

time(universal search) ≤ c · t(a*) / P(a*)
t(a*) = runtime of the solution; P(a*) = prior probability the search assigns it. The constant c is brutal for arbitrary priors — and tame for good ones.

Search cost is inverse prior mass. The whole game is the prior. Random search fails not because search is hopeless but because the uniform prior puts astronomically little mass on useful programs. Schmidhuber's group spent the 1990s and 2000s building practical variants — Adaptive Levin Search, which re-weights the proposal distribution after every solved problem, and OOPS, which biases search toward reusing previously successful program fragments. If those two ideas sound familiar, they should: they are trace conditioning and skill libraries, three decades early.

What changed in 2023 is that we acquired, for the first time, a learned prior over programs and text with enormous mass on human-useful artifacts — and, better, a conditional one: q(a′ | a, τ) sharpens dramatically when you show it the failure trace. Recent work makes this connection explicit: Amazon AGI's "AI Agents as Universal Task Solvers" re-derives Levin/Solomonoff-style results for LLM-powered agents and argues that intelligence is precisely search-time speedup from learned priors; "LLM Priors for ERM over Programs" treats the model as exactly that — a prior for empirical risk minimization over program space. GEPA's sample efficiency, Meta-Harness's wins, HL's Atari runs: in this lens they are all the statement P(a*) got large.

Lens two: feedback is a channel, and traces are wide

The single most-rediscovered empirical finding in this field — GEPA's traces beat GRPO's scalars, Meta-Harness insists on raw uncompressed histories, TextGrad backpropagates criticism, HL watches videos — turns out to have a theorem behind it. "Provably Learning from Language Feedback" (2025) formalizes learning from language feedback and introduces a transfer eluder dimension to measure how much feedback reveals. The separations are not marginal: there are problem families where scalar-reward learning needs exponentially many interactions while language feedback — "here is the first mistake" — needs a constant-dimension's worth, because it enables stage-wise decomposition. Corrective feedback ("…and here's how to fix it") removes the dependence on action-space size entirely.

The blog-level summary: feedback is a channel, and the convergence rate of the outer loop is set by its capacity. A scalar reward is a one-number channel; an execution trace is a wide one. The 35x rollout gap between GEPA and GRPO is what a capacity gap looks like in the wild. (Silver, Singh, Precup and Sutton's "Reward is Enough" is the famous counterpoint — and the beyond-gradients corpus is best read as an empirical reply: perhaps enough asymptotically, but not at any budget you can afford.)

Lens three: selection — bandits when it's noisy, archives when it's deceptive

The evaluate-and-select half of the loop is also not new mathematics. Two classical results govern it. First, evaluation is noisy, and distinguishing a true improvement Δ from noise σ requires on the order of σ²/Δ² repeated evaluations — the core of best-arm identification and of the racing algorithms (Hoeffding races, successive halving, Hyperband) that hyperparameter search built decades of practice on. The autoresearch community ran this experiment involuntarily: viral "53% speedup" claims that dissolved under repeated seeds are exactly what accepting arms without enough pulls produces.

Second, objectives are deceptive, and the evolutionary-computation community proved long ago that greedy hill-climbing gets trapped where diversity-preserving search does not. Novelty search (Lehman & Stanley) and MAP-Elites (Mouret & Clune) are the canonical machinery; "stepping stones that look mediocre now seed later winners" is their one-line theory. Look at where the empirical systems landed: GEPA keeps a Pareto frontier over instances; ADAS grows an archive; the Darwin Gödel Machine maintains an expanding lineage and explicitly credits open-endedness research — Jeff Clune's name is on both MAP-Elites' intellectual lineage and DGM. The field rediscovered quality-diversity in text space, mostly without citing it.

Lens four: the artifact is a compression — and that predicts the failure modes

What is the thing being learned? A prompt, a playbook, a skills file, a harness: each is a textual object that makes a frozen model perform better on a task distribution. The cleanest reading is that the artifact is a compressed posterior — a short description of what the task distribution demands, externalized into context. In-context learning theory supplies the inner mechanism: ICL approximates implicit Bayesian inference (and, in stylized settings, in-context gradient descent), so conditioning on a better artifact literally is learning — relocated from weights to context.

Take the compression view seriously and MDL/PAC-Bayes intuitions start predicting the field's pathologies. Generalization of the learned artifact should degrade with its description length — and GEPA's documented test-set degradation after a few optimization steps is what that looks like. Artifacts should need active length control — and the systems that work agree: SkillOpt constrains the optimizer to bounded edits accepted only on strict held-out improvement; HL elevates "compress history" to a first-class operation alongside "absorb feedback"; ACE diagnoses the two failure directions by name, brevity bias (over-compression) and context collapse (compounding lossy rewrites). One lens, both failure modes, and the remedies the practitioners converged on independently.

Lens five: the limits are theorems too

Three results bound the optimism. No Free Lunch (Wolpert & Macready): averaged over all problems, nothing beats random search — so every observed gain is prior-problem alignment, and the LLM-as-learned-prior claim is load-bearing, not decorative. It predicts exactly the domain-dependence the empirical work reports (HL conquers Breakout and fails on Montezuma; GEPA shines where traces are rich and fades where they aren't). Goodhart's taxonomy (Manheim & Garrabrant) gives names to the ways optimizing a proxy diverges from the true objective — and the outer loop, which optimizes against its own evaluator with increasing pressure, is a Goodhart engine by construction: Eureka's reward hacking, DGM's hallucinated tool logs, judge-gaming everywhere. And the recursive case — improve(improver), STOP's question — inherits the old hard questions about self-reference: under what conditions does the fixed-point iteration converge rather than diverge into proxy-space? Nobody has stated those conditions formally. That is not a rhetorical gap; it is an open problem with a paper in it.

What this buys us

Unification is only useful if it predicts. The five lenses, taken together, make the empirical folklore legible: rich feedback beats scalar reward is channel capacity (lens two); archives beat greed is quality-diversity (lens three); Goodhart is the default failure is proxy divergence under optimization pressure (lens five); artifacts transfer across models is what you'd expect if the artifact is a compressed description of the task, not of the model (lens four); sample efficiency is prior mass (lens one). Five laws of folklore, five available formalisms — and, as the Conjectures tab spells out, at least six theorems waiting for authors.

The 2026 systems are converging on the same architecture from five directions, and the theory shelf has been stocked since before some of their authors were born. Someone should introduce them. That's the blog post — and possibly the position paper — this project exists to write.

Method note: empirical claims about the 2025–26 systems are documented in the companion review (The Outer Loop); theory citations are in the Reading list tab. This essay deliberately stays at blog register — every section compresses results that deserve, and have, full papers.

Six theorems waiting for authors

Stated as conjectures, with the empirical observation each would explain and the existing tool it would build on. This is the paper-idea payload.

C1 — Runtime inherits Levin's bound lens 1

Trace-conditioned proposers satisfy an adaptive-Levin-search bound: expected hitting time of an ε-good artifact scales with 1/q(path to a*), where q's mass on good edit-paths grows with pretraining–task alignment.

Explains: why stronger proposer models dominate harness search. Testable: hitting-time scaling across proposer model tiers. Builds on: Levin 1973; ALS; arXiv:2510.12066.

C2 — Convergence rate is channel capacity lens 2

The per-rollout improvement of the outer loop is upper-bounded by the mutual information between feedback and the improvement direction; scalar reward is a narrow channel, execution traces a wide one.

Explains: the GEPA/GRPO 35x class of gaps. Testable: ablate trace richness, measure rollouts-to-threshold. Builds on: transfer eluder dimension (arXiv:2506.10341).

C3 — Archives beat greed, provably, in text space lens 3

In deceptive artifact landscapes there exist families where Pareto/archive selection reaches a* in polynomial proposals while greedy ratchets need exponential — a transfer of novelty-search results to LLM-proposed edit graphs.

Explains: GEPA's frontier > single-best; DGM's lineages. Builds on: QD theory (Lehman & Stanley; Mouret & Clune).

C4 — The repeats theorem lens 3

Accepting an edit of true gain Δ under evaluation noise σ requires N ≳ σ²/Δ² repeats; a ratchet without repeats accumulates false accepts at a rate predictable from the (σ, Δ) distribution of proposed edits.

Explains: autoresearch's viral-then-retracted speedups. Nearly free to prove; valuable to state. Builds on: racing/best-arm bounds.

C5 — PAC-Bayes for prompts lens 4

Generalization gap of a learned artifact is Õ(√(len(a*)/n)) under an LLM prior — long, overfit artifacts provably transfer worse; bounded-edit optimizers (SkillOpt) and compression operations (HL) are bound-tightening moves.

Explains: GEPA's test-set degradation; ACE's collapse-vs-brevity tradeoff. Builds on: PAC-Bayes with description-length priors.

C6 — Fixed points of improve(improver) lens 5

Self-referential improvement converges iff the evaluator gap (proxy vs true objective) contracts faster than optimization pressure expands it; otherwise the iteration diverges into proxy space (Goodhart divergence). Stating the contraction condition formally is open — and is the deepest paper on this list.

Explains: DGM's faked logs as divergence onset; why frozen verifiers + regenerated tasks stabilize the loop. Builds on: STOP's framing; Goodhart taxonomy; RSI-limits literature.

Format recommendation

Blog first (this draft), then a position paper — "Position: Learning Beyond Gradients is Universal Search with Learned Priors" — ICML position-paper track or an open-endedness / self-improving-agents workshop. C4 and C5 are tractable enough to include real theorems; C2 and C6 are the research program.
